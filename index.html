Three units of heading a frame

The ship turns at a fixed rate and there is nothing in between: the original reads paddle 0 once a frame through the monitor ROM, and anything from 100 to 154 is dead centre. Outside that, the heading moves three units and no more, whether you have nudged the dial or thrown it hard over. A full circle is 256 units, so a half turn is forty-three frames — about seventeen seconds.

That is the whole of it. The binary reads the paddle, button 0 and the keyboard, and nothing else.

THE KEYBOARD IS A PAUSE

Press any key during a flight on the original and the game stops dead until you press another one. That is not a feature written for it — it is six instructions that read the keyboard, clear the strobe and spin. Measured: with the paddle held hard over the heading advances nine units, press a key and it advances none.

A WARNING ABOUT PACE

This game is slow. One pass of the loop — read the paddle, move everything, draw the whole scene, flip the page — is about 397,000 cycles, four tenths of a second, and eighty per cent of it is the renderer.

So the game runs at 2.57 frames a second and that is not emulation overhead. The frames-a-second control is there if you would rather not play at 1983 speed — but a pass is a whole tick of the game as well as a picture, so raising it speeds the game up rather than smoothing it.

A game is five to fifteen flights

Everything outside the flight loop is Applesoft — twenty programs on the floppy, each one RUNning the next — and everything those programs and the machine code say to each other goes through thirteen bytes at $1FF3–$1FFF, just above where the binary ends. That is the entire interface. No file, no buffer, no second channel: thirteen bytes carry the wave counters, the kills, the flight count and whether the last mission was won.

THE LOOP OF IT

Clear a wave to the last enemy, hyperdrive out, dock at the starbase, bank the bonus, hyperdrive back, and do it again. Survive as many flights as the difficulty rolled for you and the last jump puts you somewhere else entirely.

HOW LONG

The target is rolled, not fixed: 5 to 7 flights on HARD, 8 to 10 on HARDER, 10 to 15 on HARDEST. The test that ends the run is >=, so flying exactly as many as asked for finishes it — a strict > would have given every difficulty one flight too many.

SCORING

300 a fighter, 600 a cruiser, 800 a base, and 10,000 for finishing it. The starbase bonus is the interesting one: each visit adds the flights so far, capped at five, to a running total that every later visit multiplies again. A won game on HARDEST comes out near 180,000, and about two thirds of that is the bonus rather than the shooting.

Where it came from

Star Destroyer is an Apple II game published in 1983 by Harvest Time Software Company and written by Mitchell Robbins — the author of Space Vikings a year earlier — and the two games run on the same engine, one version apart. The earlier disk carries LO-HI A2-3D1; this one carries A2‑3D2, subLOGIC's enhancement of it. Both load at $6000, both put the renderer first in a jump table with the cosine and sine behind it, and a third of the newer one is literally the older one's code, moved: 2,846 bytes in 67 runs, the longest of them the 269-byte signed multiply. Compared at fixed addresses the two look 2% alike, which is how that went unnoticed.

And A2-3D2 is not a drawing routine. It is an interpreter: the scene is a program, in a thirty-opcode instruction set, sitting in a file called SV DATA. Each frame the game pokes new numbers into that program's operands — an angle here, a coordinate there — and calls the engine, which runs the scene from the top and clips, projects and plots whatever it finds. The game does not know what a line is.

One call renders the whole world in about 316,000 cycles — roughly 95,000 6502 instructions, eighteen and a half video frames, three tenths of a second. The engine is synchronised to nothing at all. It runs until it is done.

The rest of the disk is of its time in the nicest way. There are twenty Applesoft programs and four that nothing ever runs: two look like earlier versions left where they fell, one is the high-score reset the author presumably typed by hand, and the fourth is not a screen at all — it is the author's own build tool, which generates one of the machine-code overlays out of its own DATA statements and BSAVEs it. One of the four binaries on this disk has its source sitting right next to it.

The ship is called the Beowulf

For most of this project the game appeared to have no fiction in it at all. Nothing names the player's ship or the enemy; searching the floppy for a name finds nothing, and the notes said so. That was wrong, and the reason it was wrong is the better finding.

Every word this game puts on screen is drawn — one shape table, and the programs hold their text as lists of shape numbers in DATA statements. There is no text on the disk to search for. The cipher turned out to be forced rather than guessed: one program converts a digit to a shape number twice by different routes, and the two agree, which pins the table to ASCII / through Z with @ left out — 43 shapes, exactly the count the table declares. Decoded, it reads back the program's own name and the words FINAL SCORE, neither of which the derivation had any say in.

The game's entire written vocabulary is those five lines plus STARBASE, DOCKING COMPLETE, SCORE, LEVEL and FINAL SCORE. It uses 22 of the 26 letters: there is no J, no Q, no X and no Z anywhere in the game.

You can watch it happen above. Leave the game alone when the page loads and the attract mode runs: the ship turns, and three times it stops and puts a line on the screen for 1.8 seconds — which is exactly FOR J = 1 TO 2000: NEXT, timed on the machine. Press a key and you skip straight past the only place the game ever explains itself, which is what almost everybody did in 1983: the text screen before it says PRESS ANY KEY TO SKIP TITLE PAGE, and the branch nobody takes is the one with the story in it.

The lesson got written down with the finding. “A search of the disk finds nothing” is not a fact about the game. It is a fact about the search.

How it was ported

Not by reading the listings and reimplementing them. The listings tell you what the author wrote; they do not tell you what the machine does — and on this disk the difference turned out to matter on nearly every page.

So the port is built against an oracle: the real game, running on an Apple II emulator inside a headless browser, driven by a script that can press keys, read memory and screenshot the hi-res page. Everything goes through two stages.

STAGE ONE — ASK

A probe asks the machine

Boot the disk, play to the thing in question, and record what actually happened — the bytes, the screen, the cycle counts. There are 38 of these.

STAGE TWO — COMPARE

A harness holds the port to it

Drive the port the same way and compare — pixel for pixel where it is a picture, value for value where it is a number, cycle for cycle where it is a sound. There are 21 of these, and all 59 have to pass.

THE RULE

A check that cannot fail is worse than none

Because it gets counted. Every harness here was made to fail on purpose before it was believed, and three of them were wrong the first time. The ways they were wrong are written down rather than quietly fixed.

Where the port and the floppy disagree, the floppy is right — including when it is obviously a bug. Several of the ones below are bugs, and the port keeps every one of them.

What the disk actually said

A sample of things that are true of the original, that nobody would have guessed from the listings, and that the port now does too.

SHOOTING

Half your shots are free

There are two shot slots and they alternate. Only the first one takes a missile off the bar; the path that fires the second never touches it. And the one it does take is a single unit because the code jumps one byte into the routine that normally subtracts four — the same four instructions that knock four off your shield take one off the missiles, and the entry point is the whole difference.

THE LAST ENEMY

The base star shoots you in the back

It is the only thing in either mission that fires at the ship at all, and it tests four candidate headings where everything else tests two. Two of the four are the reciprocal bearing — so it will shoot at you dead astern as readily as dead ahead, while still pointing the other way. Its missile also rolls as it flies. Nothing else in the game does.

THE SPEAKER

Six of the seven sounds have no sound

There is no sound chip — a sound is a list of cycle numbers and the pitch is how long the processor waits between flipping the cone. Six of the seven routines get their waveform from a shift register that nothing ever initialises, so what a sound is depends on where the last sound left it: the same routine measured 108 toggles from one starting state and 158 from another. A recording would be one performance, not the sound, so the port runs the generator instead.

THE SEVENTH

The warp buzz is however deep the stack is

The one sound with a plain waveform opens by calling a ROM routine that returns with the stack pointer in X — and X is the delay counter. So the first gap of the buzz is however deep the stack happened to be when you engaged warp. Four different stack depths give first gaps of 610, 1250, 1887 and 2377 cycles, and then 2567 every time after.

THE SCOREBOARD

Every 8 is drawn as a 9

One line of the starbase program reads IF A = 8 THEN A = 9, applied to each digit of the docking score before it is turned into a shape. A score of 800 reads 900. The shape table has a perfectly good 8 in it, and the routine that draws the level number uses it without any such guard, so it is a slip rather than a workaround. The port keeps it.

THE FINALE

The last mission is the same code, moved

The final mission looked like a second game: 1,369 instructions in a binary of its own. 1,070 of them are the main mission's, assembled at a different address — the frame loop four bytes down, the dispatch and the kills $0813 up, the sound $080E. Asking the question per routine found almost none of it. Asking it per instruction found nearly all.

Notes for the curious

The findings document is oracle/DISK_TRUTH.md and is the only trusted account of what the disk does; node oracle/run_all.mjs runs every suite. The port is an interpreter of the disk rather than a transliteration of it: the twenty Applesoft programs are run as they are, by the same chain the machine follows, and the four machine-code overlays are transliterated 6502. The one thing kept outside the disk is your high-score table, which lives in this browser, because nothing here writes to the floppy image.
